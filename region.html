FROM ROBOT TO REGION

A factory produces robots.
A network can expand its capacity.

Three cities total 600 equivalent tasks. River city receives deliveries after one cycle; Central city after three; Valley city after five. Reservations balance each city's feasible coverage. Sector scope uses the city scene's intermediate scenario. These values illustrate differences; they are not forecasts.

This scene starts with 24 robots and six completed facilities. It does not import progress from the other scenes. You can dedicate part of the fleet to construction: initially it diverts workers from production; later it opens new facilities.

Mines supply ore. Refineries turn it into material. Two material batches form a kit, and each kit becomes one robot. Construction consumes the same material: 48 batches per facility.

The same investment share of newly refined material is reserved for future construction. Each project requires 24 robot work units and accepts up to four robots. At most three projects can run simultaneously. Insufficient energy slows construction and production. Transport limits industrial flow.

The comparison starts with the same resources and allocates new robots between industry and cities using the same share. The reference does not build facilities: it eventually has robots that its machinery cannot use.

Cycles are not days or years. All rates and costs are teaching assumptions. The region groups complex processes together; it does not model shifts, failures, permits, monetary costs, specialized chemistry or equipment manufacturing. Specialized equipment and inputs are assumed to arrive from outside. Electricity is capacity per cycle, not stored in a battery. There are six plots per industry and no new regions open: growth eventually becomes limited. This is not a Tesla forecast or a GDP calculation.
